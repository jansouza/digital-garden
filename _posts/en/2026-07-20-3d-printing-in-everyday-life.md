I've already written here about [LabRax, the homelab rack I 3D
printed]({% post_url en/2026-07-12-homelab-labrax-3d-printed-rack %}). But
the truth is the printer didn't sit idle after that. Quite the opposite: it
became a tool I use practically every week, and the part I've enjoyed the
most isn't even the technology itself, it's how much it solves everyday
things.

![Bambu Lab A1 printer with colorful filament spools](/assets/images/3d-print/MVIMG_20260704_111029.jpg)

There's something satisfying about needing a specific object, designing (or
finding) the model, and a few hours later holding it in your hand, in the
exact color and size you wanted. No waiting for a delivery, no settling for
"almost fits".

## Utility: organization that fits the space

One of the uses that paid off the most was printing bathroom organizers. A
toothbrush holder, a small cotton swab jar and a shaving accessories
organizer, all in the same ribbed style and in colors that match the
decor.

![3D printed bathroom organizers, in green and brown, with a toothbrush, jars and shaving accessories](/assets/images/3d-print/MVIMG_20260712_103327.jpg)

Unlike buying something off the shelf, you can adjust it to the exact size
of the gap by the sink or on the shelf. It sounds silly, but solving that
kind of detail with a custom-made part is a big gain in convenience.

Same logic in the living room: an organizer for the remote controls, with a
slot for each one, instead of that mess on the coffee table.

![3D printed remote control organizer, in black and white](/assets/images/3d-print/IMG_20260720_141945.jpg)

## Decor: vases and sculptures

On the purely decorative side, the vases with a ribbed pattern (that
"bellows" effect) are among our favorite pieces at home. They're quick to
print, look great, and you can play with color combinations by switching
filament in the middle of the print.

![Two 3D printed vases with a ribbed pattern, one pink and one green](/assets/images/3d-print/MVIMG_20260706_143750.jpg)

![3D printed vase with an olive green ribbed pattern and terracotta feet, on a balcony](/assets/images/3d-print/IMG_20260720_142144.jpg)

I also printed some more elaborate sculptures, including replicas of
well-known Banksy works, framed on the wall like relief pictures. It's the
kind of piece I'd hardly buy ready-made, but it becomes accessible when you
have the printer at home.

![Sculptures inspired by Banksy works framed on the wall](/assets/images/3d-print/MVIMG_20260712_175018.jpg)

![Detail of Banksy's "Flower Thrower" sculpture, 3D printed](/assets/images/3d-print/MVIMG_20260712_175029.jpg)

## And there's room for fun too

Not everything needs a purpose. I printed Gollum, a Darth Vader and even a
little dog just because they're great models to test fine details and the
printer's quality on miniatures.

![3D printed Gollum miniature, in gray resin, with detailed facial expression](/assets/images/3d-print/IMG_20260709_162318.jpg)

![3D printed Darth Vader miniature, in matte black](/assets/images/3d-print/IMG_20260720_142044.jpg)

![3D printed miniature of a little dog, in black and white](/assets/images/3d-print/IMG_20260720_142001.jpg)

## What this hobby leaves me with

The technical side (slicing, choosing filament, tuning supports, speed vs.
quality) is still a constant learning process, and maybe I'll write more
about it in another post. But what has hooked me the most so far is this
mix of practical utility with a bit of art: a printer that solves an
annoying organization problem just as well as it gives you an excuse to put
art on the wall.

If you're curious about getting started, the advice is simple: don't wait
until you have the perfect project in mind. Start by printing any small,
useful thing for your home. It's the fastest way to learn the process and
gain something real along the way.
