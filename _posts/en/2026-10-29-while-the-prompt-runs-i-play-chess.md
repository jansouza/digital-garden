![Terminal with an agent running the tests next to a chessboard with the blitz clock](/assets/images/enquanto-o-prompt-roda-eu-jogo-xadrez/capa-terminal-e-tabuleiro.png)

What do you do while you wait for your prompt to run? I play chess.

I play blitz on chess.com, 5 minutes per side. A game lasts at most 10
minutes, which is roughly the time an agent takes to read the project,
change a few files and run the tests. I kick off the task, start a game
and, when it's over, I go back to see what the agent did.

## The rating

On October 23 I reached 1261 in blitz
([here's the game](https://www.chess.com/game/live/185819013938)), the
highest rating I've had since I created the account, in 2021.

For someone who plays well, that's not much. For me it's a lot. I've played
more than 9,000 blitz games, practically half won and half lost, and I
spent about two and a half years stuck around a thousand. This year the
curve finally started going up, especially from August on.

![Blitz rating chart on chess.com from December 2022 to October 2026: it rises quickly in early 2023, stays around a thousand until mid-2025 and reaches the record of 1261 on October 23, 2026](/assets/images/enquanto-o-prompt-roda-eu-jogo-xadrez/rating-blitz-chess-com.png)

I can't really explain the rise, but I know well what makes me drop. When
I'm stressed or anxious, I lose more, and I usually lose the same way, not
seeing a simple move by my opponent. The
[attentional control theory](https://tu-dresden.de/mn/psychologie/allgpsy/ressourcen/dateien/lehre/pruefungsliteratur_KN_2013/Eysenck-2007.pdf?lang=de)
(Eysenck and colleagues, 2007) says that anxiety takes up part of working
memory and gets in the way of shifting the focus of attention. It wasn't
designed for chess, but it fits the board well, where seeing the threat
requires taking your eyes off your own plan and looking at what the other
side is preparing.

## Every craft has waiting time

For me, waiting in the middle of work is new. For years programming was a
continuous activity: I thought and typed at the same time, and the code
only moved while I was at the keyboard. With agents, a gap of a few minutes
appeared between asking and receiving, in which the work happens without
me.

Outside of technology, this is old. The baker kneads the dough and waits
hours for it to rise. Meanwhile he cleans the counter and prepares the next
batch, without going far, because dough that rises too much collapses. The
painter applies the first coat and waits for it to dry before the second.
If he applies the second too soon, he ruins both.

In both cases the waiting is part of the work, and the professional knows
what to do while waiting and when to go back. With chess I solved the first
part; the second I'm still learning.

## When to go back

The agent can't be left alone for too long either. Sometimes it stops to
ask permission to run a command and sits there, waiting for me. Sometimes
it misunderstands the task and starts changing things where it shouldn't.
The longer I take to look, the more of its work goes in the trash.

Chess.com records 17 abandoned blitz games in 2026, and I dropped some of
them halfway through to go check the code. An abandoned game counts as a
loss and costs rating. Even so, when the agent needs me, the code wins.

Blitz helps because it has an end time. In 10 minutes at most, the game is
over and I go back to the terminal. With a streaming series or my phone's
feed, I wouldn't have that limit.

Some people are betting on the opposite path. In November 2025,
[Exame reported](https://exame.com/carreira/nova-ferramenta-de-programacao-que-deixa-voce-ver-tiktok-e-usar-tinder-enquanto-a-ia-trabalha/)
(in Portuguese) that Clad Labs launched "Chad: The Brainrot IDE", a code
editor that lets programmers watch TikTok, play games and use Tinder
without leaving the screen while the AI works. The creators say this
reduces the cost of context switching, because the distraction stays
inside the editor and not on the phone, full of notifications. It may even
be faster to come back, but the feed is still endless, and nothing in it
tells you when to stop, which the blitz clock does for me.

## Thinking without help

In June I wrote that
[the fear isn't AI thinking, but us no longer thinking]({% post_url en/2026-06-20-afraid-to-stop-thinking-ai-and-critical-thinking %}),
about the risk of no longer reasoning because it's comfortable to let AI do
it. Chess became a practical answer to that fear, without my having planned
it. While the agent writes the code, I spend five minutes calculating moves
on my own and against the clock. It's the kind of effort that working with
AI keeps taking out of my day, and on the board it's still mandatory.

Chess gets the gap. Architecture, data modeling and reviewing what the
agent delivered stay in work time. If the agent does everything and I spend
the day playing, then the problem is a different one.

My chess.com profile is [jansouza](https://www.chess.com/member/jansouza),
if you want a game while your prompt runs.
